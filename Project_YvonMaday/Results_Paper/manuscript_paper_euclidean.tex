\begin{abstract}
We compare learned closures and nonlinear manifolds for parametric
projection-based reduced-order models of a nonlinear time-dependent system.
All models use a common Euclidean proper-orthogonal-decomposition (POD)
coordinate system and are trained from full-residual linear
projection-based reduced-order model (PROM) trajectories.  The comparison
includes state-conditioned, parameter--time-conditioned, and hybrid
\textsc{PROM-ANN} closures; an intrusive \textsc{PROM-POD-AE} decoder
manifold; a non-intrusive \textsc{POD-NN-ROM} direct coefficient map; and a
non-intrusive latent \textsc{POD-DL-ROM}.

The numerical study uses a parametric two-dimensional inviscid Burgers
benchmark.  The intrusive models all minimize the same time-discrete residual,
but over different learned trial maps.  A shared parameter--time map gives a
controlled sweep from a non-intrusive POD--NN--ROM endpoint to the
full-coordinate linear PROM.  A complementary tail-perturbation diagnostic
quantifies how a prescribed Case--2 secondary-coordinate error transfers to
the residual-solved primary coordinates.  We then augment the prescribed
tail with a small residual-adaptive correction space.  Its directions follow
from eliminating the primary increment in the linearized LSPG problem and
are held fixed during each nonlinear time-step solve.  With three correction
directions and the unchanged baseline network, the mean in-domain state error
decreases from $1.617\%$ to $0.447\%$ in a common-initialization comparison.
A nested coefficient-data study
with 9, $9+8$, $9+12$, and $9+18$ PROM trajectories separates in-domain
improvement from extrapolation behaviour.  We also state the empirical
cubature method (ECM) formulation required for a future HPROM extension; all
numerical results reported here are full-residual PROM results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) reduces a high-dimensional model
while retaining its governing residual and its parameter dependence
\cite{antoulas2005approximation,quarteroni2014reduced}.  For nonlinear,
transport-dominated systems, an accurate linear subspace may nevertheless
require many coordinates.  Nonlinear trial manifolds, learned closures, and
latent representations seek to reduce the number of online unknowns without
discarding the projection principle
\cite{barnett2023neural,cohen2023nonlinear,aghili2026nonlinear}.

This work compares intrusive closure-enhanced PROMs (PROM--ANN), an intrusive
decoder manifold (PROM--POD--AE), a non-intrusive direct coefficient map
(POD--NN--ROM), and a non-intrusive latent map (POD--DL--ROM).  PROM means
that the full time-discrete residual is minimized online.  It is not
synonymous with a generic ROM: the non-intrusive baselines do not evaluate a
residual online.  Likewise, the HPROM formulation below is a sampled-residual
extension of PROM, not a second name for the same computation.

The reported campaign uses a standard Euclidean POD basis and a common
full-residual linear PROM teacher.  This permits a direct comparison between
a parameter--time-conditioned closure, introduced below as Case~2, which
prescribes a learned secondary-coordinate tail but solves a primary
coordinate block, and POD--NN--ROM, which predicts the full coordinate
vector from the same parameter--time map.  Hyperreduction is developed
explicitly because an ECM rule must be tied to the trial manifold and its LSPG
tangent; HPROM numerical results are deliberately deferred until the
corresponding campaign is available.

Two responses to limited regression accuracy are examined separately.  The
first adds a small number of residual-solved secondary correction amplitudes
to Case~2, using the existing trained map.  The second enriches the
linear-PROM coefficient data and retrains all learned families.  The former
spends additional work online; the latter spends additional work offline.
Their separate assessment identifies what can be recovered from the
governing equations before generating further training trajectories.

\section{Projection Framework}
\label{sec:projection-framework}
\subsection{Time-discrete residual and Euclidean POD basis}
Consider a parametric nonlinear semi-discrete initial-value problem
\begin{equation}
\mathbf M(\mub)\dot{\uvec}(t;\mub)+\mathbf f(\uvec(t;\mub);\mub)
-\mathbf g(t;\mub)=\mathbf0,\qquad \uvec(0;\mub)=\uvec_0(\mub),
\label{eq:ivp}
\end{equation}
with $\mub\in\mathcal D$.  After a chosen implicit time discretization, let
$\mathbf r^{m+1}(\uvec;\mub)\in\mathbb R^N$ denote the residual at step
$m+1$, with dependence on the previous state understood.  The HDM solves
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r^{m+1}(\uvec^{m+1};\mub)=\mathbf0.
\label{eq:discrete-residual}
\end{equation}
Let $\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be the snapshot mean and let $\mathbf S_c$ collect
the centered HDM snapshots.  The thin singular-value decomposition
\begin{equation}
\mathbf S_c=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf Y^T
\end{equation}
defines the Euclidean POD basis retained in this work,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathbf U(:,1:n_{\mathrm{tot}}),\qquad
\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}^T\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathbf I.
\label{eq:euclidean-pod}
\end{equation}
The common affine linear trial manifold is
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_N)=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+
\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}\q_N,\qquad
\q_N\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{tot}}}.
\label{eq:linear-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
All learned trial maps below are expressed in these same coordinates.

\subsection{Linear PROM and LSPG reference}
The full-residual linear PROM is the time-discrete least-squares
Petrov--Galerkin (LSPG) problem
\begin{equation}
\q_N^{m+1}(\mub)=\arg\min_{\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{tot}}}}
\norm{\mathbf r^{m+1}(\boldsymbol\phi_N(\mathbf x);\mub)}_2^2.
\label{eq:linear-prom}
\end{equation}
At Gauss--Newton iterate $k$, define
\begin{equation}
\mathbf T_N=\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}},\qquad
\mathbf J^{m+1,k}=
\left.\frac{\partial\mathbf r^{m+1}}{\partial\uvec}\right|_{
\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_N^{m+1,k})},\qquad
\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}=\mathbf J^{m+1,k}\mathbf T_N.
\end{equation}
The normal equations for the increment are
\begin{equation}
\left(\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}
\Delta\q_N^{m+1,k}=-
\left(\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T
\mathbf r^{m+1}\!\left(\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_N^{m+1,k});\mub\right).
\label{eq:linear-prom-gn}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:linear-prom} generates all training targets in the PROM
campaign.

\subsection{Linear HPROM and ECM formulation}
\label{sec:linear-hprom}
The linear HPROM replaces full residual evaluations in the LSPG update by a
sparse, positive empirical-cubature approximation.  This formulation is
given in detail because the same projected-residual algebra is used when the
affine trial manifold is replaced by a learned one.  Let
$\mathcal E=\{e_i\}_{i=1}^{N_e}$ be the mesh entities.  The time-discrete
residual is assembled from local contributions as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r^{m+1}(\uvec;\mub)=\sum_{e_i\in\mathcal E}\mathbf L_{e_i}^T
\mathbf r_{e_i}^{m+1}(\mathbf L_{e_i^+}\uvec;\mub).
\label{eq:entity-residual}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf r_{e_i}^{m+1}\in\mathbb R^{d_{e_i}}$ is the contribution of
entity $e_i$, $\mathbf L_{e_i}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{e_i}\times N}$ extracts its
residual degrees of freedom, and $\mathbf L_{e_i^+}$ extracts the enlarged
state stencil needed to evaluate it.  The stencil contains neighbouring
entities whenever the discretization requires them.

For the affine trial map \eqref{eq:linear-reconstruction}, the projected
LSPG residual at a Gauss--Newton iterate has the entity-wise form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf r_N^{m+1,k}(\mub)
&:=\left(\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T
\mathbf r^{m+1}\!\left(\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_N^{m+1,k});\mub\right)\\
&=\sum_{e_i\in\mathcal E}
\left(\mathbf L_{e_i}\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T
\mathbf r_{e_i}^{m+1}\!\left(
\mathbf L_{e_i^+}\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_N^{m+1,k});\mub\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:linear-projected-residual}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathbf J_{e_i}^{m+1,k}$ be the derivative of the local residual with
respect to the stencil state at the same iterate.  The Gauss--Newton matrix
is assembled analogously,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf J_N^{m+1,k}:=
\left(\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T\mathbf J^{m+1,k}\mathbf T_N
=\sum_{e_i\in\mathcal E}
\left(\mathbf L_{e_i}\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T
\mathbf J_{e_i}^{m+1,k}\left(\mathbf L_{e_i^+}\mathbf T_N\right).
\label{eq:linear-projected-jacobian}
\end{equation}

ECM selects a reduced entity set $\widetilde{\mathcal E}_N\subset\mathcal E$
and positive weights $\xi_{N,e_i}$.  The same rule approximates both
projected quantities,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathbf r}_N^{m+1,k}
&=\sum_{e_i\in\widetilde{\mathcal E}_N}\xi_{N,e_i}
\left(\mathbf L_{e_i}\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T
\mathbf r_{e_i}^{m+1}\!\left(
\mathbf L_{e_i^+}\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_N^{m+1,k});\mub\right),\\
\widehat{\mathbf J}_N^{m+1,k}
&=\sum_{e_i\in\widetilde{\mathcal E}_N}\xi_{N,e_i}
\left(\mathbf L_{e_i}\mathbf W_N^{m+1,k}\right)^T
\mathbf J_{e_i}^{m+1,k}\left(\mathbf L_{e_i^+}\mathbf T_N\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:linear-ecm-sampled-normal-equations}
\end{equation}
The online sampled update is consequently
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf J}_N^{m+1,k}\Delta\q_N^{m+1,k}
=-\widehat{\mathbf r}_N^{m+1,k},\qquad
\q_N^{m+1,k+1}=\q_N^{m+1,k}+\Delta\q_N^{m+1,k}.
\label{eq:linear-hprom-gn-update}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Offline cubature construction.}
For every selected parameter--time training state $s$, the linear coordinate
$\q_{N,s}$ and its LSPG test basis $\mathbf W_{N,s}$ define the contribution
of entity $e_i$ to the projected residual,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf c_{N,e_i}^{(s)}=(\mathbf L_{e_i}\mathbf W_{N,s})^T
\mathbf r_{e_i}(\mathbf L_{e_i^+}\boldsymbol\phi_N(\q_{N,s});\mub_s).
\label{eq:linear-ecm-contribution}
\end{equation}
Stacking all $n_{\mathrm{tot}}$-component contributions over
$N_{\mathrm{ECM}}$ selected states gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C_N=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf c_{N,e_1}^{(1)} & \cdots & \mathbf c_{N,e_{N_e}}^{(1)}\\
\vdots & & \vdots\\
\mathbf c_{N,e_1}^{(N_{\mathrm{ECM}})} & \cdots &
\mathbf c_{N,e_{N_e}}^{(N_{\mathrm{ECM}})}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{tot}}N_{\mathrm{ECM}}\times N_e},
\qquad \mathbf b_N=\mathbf C_N\mathbf1.
\label{eq:linear-ecm-training-matrix}
\end{equation}
ECM seeks a sparse nonnegative approximation of the full projected-residual
data,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\xi_N=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\zeta\geq\mathbf0}
\norm{\mathbf C_N\boldsymbol\zeta-\mathbf b_N}_2^2,\qquad
\widetilde{\mathcal E}_N=\{e_i\in\mathcal E:\xi_{N,e_i}>0\}.
\label{eq:linear-ecm}
\end{equation}
In the present implementation, a direct SVD removes numerically null
directions of $\mathbf C_N$ before the nonnegative cubature solve.  The rule
is therefore tied to the affine trial manifold and its LSPG test basis.  In
particular, replacing $\boldsymbol\phi_N$ by a learned closure or decoder
also changes the tangent, the test basis, and the projected contributions;
the corresponding ECM rule must be constructed for that map.

\paragraph{Relation to other hyperreduction methods.}
The common step is to approximate an entity-wise sum of \emph{projected}
residual contributions, rather than the state reconstruction or a learned
map.  We use ECM in the sense of empirical cubature
\cite{hernandez2017dimensional,hernandez2020multiscale}.  ECSW is another
project--then--approximate hyperreduction strategy
\cite{farhat2015structure}.  It could be used with the same LSPG and trial
manifold formulations, but ECM is the rule used in this work.

\section{Learned Reduced Representations}
\label{sec:learned-representations}
\subsection{PROM--ANN closures}
Partition the common POD basis and coordinate vector as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}=[\mathbf V\ \overline{\mathbf V}],\qquad
\q_N=\begin{bmatrix}\qp\\\qs\end{bmatrix},\qquad
n+\bar n=n_{\mathrm{tot}}.
\label{eq:partition}
\end{equation}
Here $\q\in\mathbb R^n$ and $\qbar\in\mathbb R^{\bar n}$ denote the
primary and secondary coordinates, respectively.  The Euclidean POD
construction gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V^T\mathbf V=\mathbf I_n,\qquad
\overline{\mathbf V}^{\,T}\overline{\mathbf V}=\mathbf I_{\bar n},\qquad
\mathbf V^T\overline{\mathbf V}=\mathbf0,
\label{eq:partition-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
and the associated linear state is
\begin{equation}
\uvec=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+\mathbf V\q+
\overline{\mathbf V}\qbar.
\label{eq:partitioned-state}
\end{equation}
The full-coordinate linear PROM solves both blocks.  A learned closure
instead supplies the secondary block and leaves only the primary block as
an online unknown.
PROM--ANN supplies the secondary block through a learned relation
\begin{equation}
\qs=\mathcal F(\qp,\mub,t),\qquad
\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathcal F}(\qp;\mub,t)=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+
\mathbf V\qp+\overline{\mathbf V}\mathcal F(\qp,\mub,t).
\label{eq:closure-manifold}
\end{equation}
The network provides reduced secondary coordinates, not a direct HDM state
approximation.  The primary coordinates remain online unknowns of a residual
minimization.  This is the neural-network-augmented projection construction
of \cite{barnett2023neural}; analogous closure roles have also been
represented by non-neural regressors \cite{ares2026closure}.

The three cases are information patterns in this common construction:
\begin{align}
\text{Case 1:}\quad&\qs=\mathcal N(\qp),&
\text{Case 2:}\quad&\qs=\mathcal M(\mub,t),&
\text{Case 3:}\quad&\qs=\mathcal H(\qp,\mub,t).
\label{eq:closure-cases}
\end{align}
Case~1 is state-conditioned, Case~2 parameter--time-conditioned, and Case~3
combines both inputs.  The LSPG problem is
\begin{equation}
\qp^{m+1}(\mub)=\arg\min_{\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n}
\norm{\mathbf r^{m+1}(\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathcal F}
(\mathbf x;\mub,t^{m+1});\mub)}_2^2,
\label{eq:closure-lspg}
\end{equation}
with tangent
\begin{equation}
\mathbf T_{\mathcal F}=\mathbf V+\overline{\mathbf V}
\frac{\partial\mathcal F}{\partial\qp},\qquad
\mathbf W_{\mathcal F}=\mathbf J\mathbf T_{\mathcal F}.
\label{eq:closure-tangent}
\end{equation}
In particular, at a Gauss--Newton iterate the coordinate increment solves
\begin{equation}
\Delta\q=\arg\min_{\mathbf d\in\mathbb R^n}
\norm{\mathbf r+\mathbf J\mathbf T_{\mathcal F}\mathbf d}_2^2,
\qquad
\mathbf W_{\mathcal F}^T\mathbf W_{\mathcal F}\Delta\q
=-\mathbf W_{\mathcal F}^T\mathbf r.
\label{eq:closure-gn}
\end{equation}
The residual, Jacobian, and tangent are all evaluated at that iterate;
the previous time-discrete state remains fixed during the nonlinear solve.
The normal equations express the optimality condition, while the numerical
implementation solves the least-squares system directly.
For Case~2, $\partial\mathcal M/\partial\qp=\mathbf0$ and
$\mathbf T_{\mathcal M}=\mathbf V$.  Its tangent is therefore the cheapest,
but its prescribed secondary block is not corrected through a
state-dependent tangent.

\subsection{PROM--POD--AE and intrusive trial maps}
An autoencoder maps the complete linear coordinate vector through a latent
variable,
\begin{equation}
\z=\mathcal E(\q_N),\qquad
\widehat{\q}_N=\mathcal D(\z),\qquad \z\in\mathbb R^{n_z}.
\end{equation}
PROM--POD--AE uses the decoder as an intrusive nonlinear trial manifold,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{AE}}(\z)=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+
\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}\mathcal D(\z),\qquad
\mathbf T_{\mathrm{AE}}=\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}
\frac{\partial\mathcal D}{\partial\z}.
\label{eq:podae-manifold}
\end{equation}
Its latent coordinate minimizes the same residual as
\eqref{eq:closure-lspg}, but over $\z$ rather than $\qp$.  This is distinct
from POD--DL--ROM, which is non-intrusive.

\subsection{Model-specific ECM construction}
\label{sec:nonlinear-ecm}
The HPROM extension does not reuse the linear rule in \eqref{eq:linear-ecm}.
For any intrusive map $\boldsymbol\phi_c$ and tangent $\mathbf T_c$, the
entity contributions use $\mathbf W_c=\mathbf J\mathbf T_c$.  A manifold
coordinate is first fitted at every selected HDM state,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y_{c,s}=\arg\min_{\mathbf y}\norm{\uvec_s^{m_s+1}-
\boldsymbol\phi_c(\mathbf y;\mub_s,t^{m_s+1})}_2^2.
\label{eq:nonlinear-manifold-fit}
\end{equation}
For Case~2 this is linear least squares after subtracting the prescribed tail,
\begin{equation}
{\qp}_{2,s}=\mathbf V^\dagger\left[
\uvec_s^{m_s+1}-\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}-
\overline{\mathbf V}\mathcal M(\mub_s,t^{m_s+1})\right].
\label{eq:case2-ecm-fit}
\end{equation}
For Cases~1 and~3 and for the decoder manifold, it is nonlinear least
squares, for example with the Gauss--Newton update
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y_{c,s}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf y_{c,s}^{(k)}-
\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol\phi_c}{\partial\mathbf y}\right]^\dagger
\left[\boldsymbol\phi_c(\mathbf y_{c,s}^{(k)};\mub_s,t^{m_s+1})-
\uvec_s^{m_s+1}\right].
\label{eq:nonlinear-manifold-fit-gn}
\end{equation}
At the fitted state,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf c_{c,e_i}^{(s)}=(\mathbf L_{e_i}\mathbf W_{c,s})^T
\mathbf r_{e_i}(\mathbf L_{e_i^+}
\boldsymbol\phi_c(\mathbf y_{c,s};\mub_s,t^{m_s+1});\mub_s).
\label{eq:nonlinear-ecm-contribution}
\end{equation}
Stacking these vectors produces $\mathbf C_c$ and
$\mathbf b_c=\mathbf C_c\mathbf1$ as in \eqref{eq:linear-ecm}; a positive
ECM rule is then computed for each map.  The trained closure or decoder, its
tangent, and the parameter--time sample set are therefore part of a rule.  A
linear-HPROM rule, or one built for a different nonlinear map, cannot be
reused \cite{hernandez2017dimensional,hernandez2020multiscale,de2023hyper}.

\subsection{Non-intrusive reference models}
POD--NN--ROM is a direct map
\begin{equation}
\q_N(t;\mub)\approx\mathcal G_q(\mub,t),
\label{eq:podnn}
\end{equation}
and does not solve a residual online.  POD--DL--ROM uses a learned latent map
and decoder,
\begin{equation}
\z(t;\mub)\approx\mathcal G_z(\mub,t),\qquad
\q_N(t;\mub)\approx\mathcal D(\mathcal G_z(\mub,t)),
\label{eq:poddl}
\end{equation}
following the POD--DL--ROM principle \cite{fresca2022pod}.  These are direct
reduced-coordinate regressors, not PROM residual solves.

\section{Residual-Adaptive Secondary Correction for Case~2}
\label{sec:adaptive-case2}
\subsection{From a prescribed tail to a corrected trial space}
In Case~2, the network fixes $\qbar=\mathcal M(\mub,t)$ and LSPG adjusts
only $\q$.  At a fixed linearization, primary increments change the residual
only within $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf J\mathbf V)$.  The component of a
secondary prediction error's linearized residual contribution orthogonal to
that space cannot be removed by primary increments alone.  The proposed extension retains
the trained map and permits selected secondary corrections online.

At step $m+1$, let $\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\in\mathbb R^{\bar n\times r}$
contain $r$ orthonormal directions in secondary-coordinate space, where
$0\leq r\leq\bar n$.  Their construction is given below.  Introduce
$r$ additional amplitudes $\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^r$ and define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\qbar&=\mathcal M(\mub,t^{m+1})+\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\mathbf a,\\
\boldsymbol\phi_r^{m+1}(\q,\mathbf a;\mub)
&=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+\mathbf V\q+
\overline{\mathbf V}
\left[\mathcal M(\mub,t^{m+1})+\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\mathbf a\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:adaptive-trial}
\end{equation}
Each column of $\mathbf B_r^{m+1}$ is a pattern over all $\bar n$
secondary coordinates.  Thus $r$ counts independent correction patterns,
not selected POD coefficients.  A single amplitude can modify every
secondary coordinate through its corresponding column.

The complete coordinate vector retains the notation of the linear
reference:
\begin{equation}
\q_N^{m+1}=\begin{bmatrix}
\q^{m+1}\\
\mathcal M(\mub,t^{m+1})+\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\mathbf a^{m+1}
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{tot}}},\qquad
\uvec_r^{m+1}=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}\q_N^{m+1}.
\label{eq:adaptive-complete-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Thus $\q_N$ always denotes the complete reconstructed reduced-coordinate
vector; $[\q;\mathbf a]$ contains only the independent online unknowns.

Once $\mathbf B_r^{m+1}$ has been constructed and held fixed, the map
\eqref{eq:adaptive-trial} is affine in its $n+r$ unknowns.  Its exact tangent
is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf T_r^{m+1}=\left[\mathbf V\quad
\overline{\mathbf V}\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\right],\qquad
(\mathbf T_r^{m+1})^T\mathbf T_r^{m+1}=\mathbf I_{n+r}.
\label{eq:adaptive-tangent}
\end{equation}
The second identity follows from \eqref{eq:partition-orthogonality} and
$(\mathbf B_r^{m+1})^T\mathbf B_r^{m+1}=\mathbf I_r$.  It also gives
$\norm{\overline{\mathbf V}\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\mathbf a}_2
=\norm{\mathbf a}_2$: the amplitude norm measures the state correction in
the Euclidean norm already used by the POD basis.

\subsection{Eliminating the primary increment}
The correction directions should address residual components that the
primary update cannot remove.  To identify them, first form the predictor
\begin{equation}
\uvec_*^{m+1}=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+\mathbf V\q^m+
\overline{\mathbf V}\mathcal M(\mub,t^{m+1}).
\label{eq:adaptive-predictor}
\end{equation}
The residual at this predictor uses the previous \emph{computed} state
$\uvec_r^m$ in its time-discretization terms.  No reference trajectory enters
that evaluation.  In the derivation that follows, suppress $m+1$ and
$\mub$ and write
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r_* = \mathbf r^{m+1}(\uvec_*^{m+1};\mub),\qquad
\mathbf J_* = \left.\frac{\partial\mathbf r^{m+1}}{\partial\uvec}
\right|_{\uvec_*^{m+1}},\qquad \mathbf A=\mathbf J_*\mathbf V.
\label{eq:adaptive-linearization}
\end{equation}
If both coordinate blocks were free, the linearized correction would solve
\begin{equation}
\min_{\Delta\q\in\mathbb R^n,\,\Delta\qbar\in\mathbb R^{\bar n}}
\norm{\mathbf r_*+\mathbf A\Delta\q+
\mathbf J_*\overline{\mathbf V}\Delta\qbar}_2^2.
\label{eq:adaptive-full-linearized}
\end{equation}
Assume that $\mathbf A$ has full column rank and take its thin QR
factorization $\mathbf A=\mathbf Q\mathbf R_A$, with
$\mathbf Q^T\mathbf Q=\mathbf I_n$.  Define the orthogonal projector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf P=\mathbf I_N-\mathbf Q\mathbf Q^T.
\label{eq:adaptive-projector}
\end{equation}
For a fixed $\Delta\qbar$, least squares eliminates the primary increment:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\q(\Delta\qbar)=-\mathbf A^\dagger
\left(\mathbf r_*+\mathbf J_*\overline{\mathbf V}\Delta\qbar\right).
\label{eq:adaptive-primary-elimination}
\end{equation}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:adaptive-full-linearized} leaves precisely
\begin{equation}
\min_{\Delta\qbar\in\mathbb R^{\bar n}}
\norm{\mathbf P\mathbf r_*+\mathbf C\Delta\qbar}_2^2,
\qquad \mathbf C=\mathbf P\mathbf J_*\overline{\mathbf V}.
\label{eq:adaptive-secondary-ls}
\end{equation}
The projector is essential: a direction is assessed by its effect on the
residual \emph{after} the best primary correction, rather than by its effect
on a residual component already removable through $\mathbf V$.

Define the symmetric positive-semidefinite operator and right-hand side
\begin{equation}
\mathbf H=\mathbf C^T\mathbf C,\qquad
\mathbf g=\mathbf C^T\mathbf P\mathbf r_*
=\overline{\mathbf V}^{\,T}\mathbf J_*^T\mathbf P\mathbf r_*.
\label{eq:adaptive-schur}
\end{equation}
Then the unconstrained secondary normal equations are
$\mathbf H\Delta\qbar=-\mathbf g$.  Equivalently, $\mathbf H$ is the
Schur complement obtained by eliminating the primary block of the full
linearized normal equations.  It is positive definite if
$\mathbf J_*\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}$ has full column rank.  These
operators are derived from the current Euclidean LSPG problem; they do not
alter the POD basis or the offline training targets.

\subsection{A small Krylov space for the secondary correction}
Solving \eqref{eq:adaptive-secondary-ls} for all $\bar n$ coordinates
would undo the reduction achieved by the closure.  Instead, for $r\geq1$
and before any Krylov breakdown, choose
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{range}(\mathbf B_r)=\mathcal K_r(\mathbf H,\mathbf g)
:=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf g,\mathbf H\mathbf g,\ldots,
\mathbf H^{r-1}\mathbf g\},\qquad \mathbf B_r^T\mathbf B_r=\mathbf I_r.
\label{eq:adaptive-krylov}
\end{equation}
Here $r$ is a user-selected computational budget.  Three directions are
one possible choice, not a restriction of the formulation.  The construction
uses orthogonalized operator applications, so it does not explicitly form
powers of $\mathbf H$.

For $r=1$ and $\mathbf g\ne\mathbf0$, the space is the span of
the negative gradient of one half of the objective in
\eqref{eq:adaptive-secondary-ls} at zero secondary increment.  The
linearized minimizer along this direction is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\qbar_1=-\frac{\mathbf g^T\mathbf g}
{\mathbf g^T\mathbf H\mathbf g}\,\mathbf g.
\label{eq:adaptive-rank-one}
\end{equation}
Indeed, inserting $\Delta\qbar=-\alpha\mathbf g$ in
\eqref{eq:adaptive-secondary-ls} and differentiating with respect to
$\alpha$ gives \eqref{eq:adaptive-rank-one}.  Because
$\mathbf g\in\operatorname{range}(\mathbf C^T)=\ker(\mathbf C)^\perp$,
a nonzero $\mathbf g$ satisfies
$\mathbf g^T\mathbf H\mathbf g=\norm{\mathbf C\mathbf g}_2^2>0$;
no assumption that the residual can be reduced to zero is needed.
Higher ranks permit an optimal combination of additional directions
$\mathbf H\mathbf g,\mathbf H^2\mathbf g,\ldots$ in the same
linearized problem.  For a fixed predictor their spaces are nested, hence
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^{r+1}}
\norm{\mathbf P\mathbf r_*+\mathbf C\mathbf B_{r+1}\mathbf a}_2
\leq
\min_{\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^r}
\norm{\mathbf P\mathbf r_*+\mathbf C\mathbf B_r\mathbf a}_2.
\label{eq:adaptive-nested-bound}
\end{equation}
This is a statement about the linearized residual at the same predictor.
It is not an error bound against the HDM and does not imply monotone
trajectory accuracy as $r$ increases.

The operator actions can be evaluated without assembling either
$\mathbf C$ or the $N\times\bar n$ product
$\mathbf J_*\overline{\mathbf V}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf P\mathbf y&=\mathbf y-\mathbf Q(\mathbf Q^T\mathbf y),\\
\mathbf H\mathbf v&=\overline{\mathbf V}^{\,T}\mathbf J_*^T
\mathbf P\left[\mathbf J_*(\overline{\mathbf V}\mathbf v)\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:adaptive-actions}
\end{equation}
The implementation constructs the orthonormal columns by Arnoldi
orthogonalization with two reorthogonalization passes.  If $\mathbf g$
is numerically zero, no secondary direction is added.  If a later vector
is numerically dependent, the construction stops and uses the attained
rank $r_m\leq r$.  In that event all formulas use $r_m$ in place of $r$;
a request for $r$ directions does not require adding null or dependent ones.

\subsection{Nonlinear solve on the frozen affine space}
The linearized problem is used to \emph{select directions}.  The accepted
time-step solution is obtained by minimizing the original nonlinear
time-discrete residual on \eqref{eq:adaptive-trial}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\q^{m+1}\\\mathbf a^{m+1}\end{bmatrix}
=\arg\min_{\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n,\,\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^{r_m}}
\norm{\mathbf r^{m+1}
(\boldsymbol\phi_r^{m+1}(\mathbf x,\mathbf b;\mub);\mub)}_2^2.
\label{eq:adaptive-nonlinear-lspg}
\end{equation}
Writing $\mathbf z=[\q;\mathbf a]$, the Gauss--Newton increment is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf z^{m+1,k}
=\arg\min_{\mathbf d\in\mathbb R^{n+r_m}}
\norm{\mathbf r^{m+1,k}+
\mathbf J^{m+1,k}\mathbf T_r^{m+1}\mathbf d}_2^2.
\label{eq:adaptive-gn}
\end{equation}
Although $\mathbf J^{m+1,k}$ changes with the iterate,
$\mathbf B_r^{m+1}$ and $\mathbf T_r^{m+1}$ remain fixed throughout
this solve.  Consequently \eqref{eq:adaptive-tangent} is the exact
derivative of the trial map being optimized.  The method does not omit a
derivative of a state-dependent $\mathbf B_r$: constructing the space and
solving on that fixed space are two distinct operations.

The complete online step is as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Predict $\mathcal M(\mub,t^{m+1})$ with the trained network and form
the predictor \eqref{eq:adaptive-predictor} from the preceding primary
coordinates $\q^m$.
\item Evaluate $\mathbf r_*$ and $\mathbf J_*$ using the preceding
computed full reduced state $\uvec_r^m$ in the time-discrete residual.
\item Factor $\mathbf J_*\mathbf V$, apply
\eqref{eq:adaptive-actions}, and construct up to $r$ columns of
$\mathbf B_r^{m+1}$ according to \eqref{eq:adaptive-krylov}.
\item Form $\mathbf T_r^{m+1}$ and initialize the nonlinear solve with
$\mathbf z^{m+1,0}=[\q^m;\mathbf0]$.  Solve
\eqref{eq:adaptive-nonlinear-lspg} by \eqref{eq:adaptive-gn}.
\item Store $\q^{m+1}$ and the corrected secondary coordinates
$\qbar^{m+1}=\mathcal M(\mub,t^{m+1})+
\mathbf B_r^{m+1}\mathbf a^{m+1}$.  Their reconstructed state is the
predecessor used at the next time step.
\end{enumerate}
At the next step a new correction space is constructed.  Its amplitudes
are initialized to zero because the columns generally differ from those at
the previous step; the previous correction is nevertheless retained through
the time-discrete predecessor state.

\subsection{Interpretation, limiting cases, and cost}
The method changes the online trial space while retaining the trained
parameter--time map.  Increasing $n$ would instead free additional individual
POD coordinates.  Increasing $r$ keeps the same primary/secondary split and
frees combinations of secondary coordinates selected by the current
residual and Jacobian.  The two choices can therefore give different
accuracy at the same number of online unknowns.

For $r=0$, \eqref{eq:adaptive-trial} is the original Case--2 map, and its
online trajectory is recovered when the initialization and nonlinear solver
settings also match.  If the constructed columns span the entire secondary
space, the corrected affine space equals the full linear-PROM space:
\begin{equation}
\left\{\boldsymbol\phi_{\bar n}^{m+1}(\q,\mathbf a;\mub)\right\}
=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+\operatorname{range}(\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}).
\label{eq:adaptive-full-space}
\end{equation}
The network offset then lies inside the freely variable linear space.
Equality of converged trajectories additionally requires compatible initial
states and selection of the same time-step minimizers.  A smaller Krylov
space can solve the predictor's linearized problem exactly upon breakdown,
without spanning the full secondary space; that fact does not make the
subsequent nonlinear problems identical.

At a fixed predecessor, the uncorrected Case--2 space is contained in the
corrected space by taking $\mathbf a=\mathbf0$.  Its global residual
minimum therefore cannot increase upon enlargement.  In practice,
Gauss--Newton is stopped at finite tolerances, and different trajectories
produce different predecessor states.  Neither space inclusion nor
\eqref{eq:adaptive-nested-bound} guarantees lower state error over a full
trajectory, nonlinear convergence, or superiority to Cases~1 and~3.

Finally, $n+r_m$ unknowns do not imply the cost of a fixed
$(n+r_m)$-mode linear PROM.  Construction requires the full residual and
Jacobian, a QR factorization of $\mathbf J_*\mathbf V$, and actions
involving all $\bar n$ secondary modes.  The final nonlinear solves use
$\mathbf J\mathbf T_r$, whose width is only $n+r_m$, and need no
network derivative with respect to the primary coordinates.  Both the
direction-construction cost and the nonlinear solve must be included in
online timings.  The present construction is a full-residual PROM method;
its adaptive spaces have not been equipped with an ECM rule in this study.

\section{Benchmark and PROM Protocol}
\label{sec:benchmark}
\subsection{Burgers problem and discretization}
We use the two-dimensional parametric inviscid Burgers benchmark of
\cite{barnett2023neural},
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathbf U}{\partial t}+
\frac{\partial\mathbf F(\mathbf U)}{\partial x}+
\frac{\partial\mathbf G(\mathbf U)}{\partial y}=\mathbf S(x;\mub),\qquad
\mathbf U=\begin{bmatrix}u_x\\u_y\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(\mathbf U)=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12u_x^2\\\tfrac12u_xu_y\end{bmatrix},
\quad\mathbf G(\mathbf U)=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12u_xu_y\\\tfrac12u_y^2\end{bmatrix},
\quad\mathbf S(x;\mub)=\begin{bmatrix}0.02\exp(\mu_2x)\\0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The domain is $[0,100]^2$, $T_f=25$, and
$\mathcal D=[4.25,5.50]\times[0.015,0.03]$.  The HDM uses a cell-centered
finite-volume Godunov discretization on a $250\times250$ grid
($N=125{,}000$) and $500$ uniform time steps with $\Delta t=0.05$.

\subsection{Data, validation, and online points}
Nine HDM trajectories on a $3\times3$ tensor grid define the centered
snapshot matrix for the Euclidean POD.  We retain $n_{\mathrm{tot}}=151$
modes, which capture $99.9900\%$ of its energy.  At these same nine parameters
we solve the full-residual linear PROM.  The resulting PROM-generated
trajectories, rather than HDM snapshots orthogonally projected after the fact,
are the learning targets for every model.  This comparison-control choice
aligns every learned model with the reduced optimality problem used as its
teacher; it is not the conventional purely non-intrusive workflow, which
typically uses projected HDM labels.

Two complete 151-coordinate linear-PROM trajectories are reserved before
training,
\begin{equation}
\mub^{\mathrm{val},1}=(4.5625,0.02625),\qquad
\mub^{\mathrm{val},2}=(5.1875,0.01875).
\label{eq:validation-points}
\end{equation}
Because each held-out parameter contains all time samples, this is a
parameter-generalization test rather than a random time-snapshot split.  The
four online evaluations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mub^{(v)}&=(4.875,0.0225),&
\mub^{(1)}&=(4.560,0.0190),\\
\mub^{(2)}&=(5.190,0.0260),&
\mub^{(3)}&=(4.000,0.0330).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:evaluation-points}
\end{equation}
The first three are in-domain and $\mub^{(3)}$ is extrapolatory.  The
validation locations are neither training nor online evaluation points.

\subsection{Protocol for the residual-adaptive comparison}
\label{sec:adaptive-protocol}
The correction experiment uses the unchanged baseline master ANN trained
from the nine linear-PROM trajectories, with the two validation parameters
in \eqref{eq:validation-points}.  It neither generates additional training
trajectories nor retrains the network.  In particular, the online directions
are constructed from the current computed state, residual, and Jacobian,
not from HDM or linear-PROM solutions at the evaluation parameters.

A preliminary local screening compared $r=1,3,5$ at the two validation
parameters.  Their mean full-trajectory coefficient errors against the
linear PROM were $1.988\%$, $1.093\%$, and $1.123\%$, respectively.
The resulting choice $r=3$ was frozen before evaluation at the four reporting
parameters.  These two validation parameters also serve network selection;
they are not an independent test set.  The rank screening used the original
ANN-offset initial representation.  The subsequent common-initialization
comparison below retained the selected rank rather than selecting it again.
Thus $r=3$ is the tested configuration, not a claim of optimal rank for every
initialization or parameter.

The dedicated Sherlock comparison isolates the online correction using a
common initial state for all four methods.  Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\q_N^0=\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}^T
\left(\uvec_0-\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),\qquad
\uvec_{\mathrm{ROM}}^0=\uvec_{\mathrm{ref}}+
\mathbf V_{\mathrm{tot}}\q_N^0.
\label{eq:adaptive-common-initial}
\end{equation}
This state need not belong to each learned trial manifold at $t=0$; it is
used as the predecessor for the first time step.  Subsequent states are
computed on the respective trial maps.  This uses the prescribed initial
condition, not future reference snapshots.  The uncorrected Case--2
trajectory is therefore recomputed with this initialization and is not
interchanged with the ANN-initialized baseline in
Section~\ref{sec:prom-results}.

Here $n_{\mathrm{tot}}=151$, $n=10$, and $\bar n=141$.  Consequently,
$\mathbf B_3^{m+1}$ has size $141\times3$ and the nonlinear solve has
$10+3=13$ unknowns, while $\q_N$ still has 151 entries.  The attained rank
was three at every step of all four reported trajectories.  All methods use
500 time steps and the same nominal Gauss--Newton settings: at most 20
iterations, a relative residual-norm threshold of $10^{-5}$, and a
relative residual-change stopping threshold of $10^{-2}$.  The native
implementations are retained: the networks are evaluated in single
precision, Cases~1 and~3 use their neural closure/tangent path, and Case~2
and its corrected variant perform the affine reduced solve in double
precision.  The timings therefore compare these implementations, not
operation counts alone.

Four methods at four parameters were run sequentially on the same Sherlock
node, equipped with Intel Xeon Gold 5118 processors, with 24 numerical
threads.  There is one measured full trajectory per method and parameter,
and no full-trajectory warmup.  The timed interval includes initialization,
network prediction, direction and tangent construction when applicable,
and time integration; it excludes model/data loading, accuracy evaluation,
and result-file writing.  The reported mean is a mean over the three
in-domain parameters, not over repeated timings.  No matched HDM timing is
available for this experiment, so no HDM-relative speed-up is inferred.

\subsection{Reported errors}
For each evaluation parameter, the state error is
\begin{equation}
e_u(\mub)=100\frac{\norm{\uvec_{\mathrm{HDM}}-\uvec_{\mathrm{ROM}}}_F}
{\norm{\uvec_{\mathrm{HDM}}}_F},
\label{eq:state-error}
\end{equation}
and the coefficient error against the full linear PROM trajectory is
\begin{equation}
e_q(\mub)=100\frac{\norm{\q_N^{\mathrm{lin}}-\widehat{\q}_N}_F}
{\norm{\q_N^{\mathrm{lin}}}_F}.
\label{eq:coefficient-error}
\end{equation}
The linear PROM is an accuracy reference for the fixed basis and full
residual, not a universal lower bound with respect to the HDM.

\subsection{Controlled Case--2 tail perturbation}
\label{sec:tail-perturbation}
To test the transfer mechanism independently of a regression magnitude, we
start from the linear-PROM secondary history $\qs^{\mathrm{lin}}$ and add a
perturbation in the direction of the actual master-map secondary error:
\begin{equation}
{\qs}_{\epsilon}=\qs^{\mathrm{lin}}+
\frac{\epsilon}{100}\frac{\widehat{\qs}-\qs^{\mathrm{lin}}}
{\norm{\widehat{\qs}-\qs^{\mathrm{lin}}}_F}
\norm{\qs^{\mathrm{lin}}}_F.
\label{eq:tail-perturbation}
\end{equation}
For each prescribed percentage $\epsilon$, Case~2 solves only the
first ten coordinates.  Thus the diagnostic uses the observed ANN-error
direction but does not conflate its effect with a change of learned model.
Table~\ref{tab:tail-perturbation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:tail-perturbation} show a
monotone transfer from secondary-tail error to the recovered primary block.
The amount of transfer is parameter dependent, so a tail loss alone does not
imply a pointwise primary-coordinate bound.

The additional accuracy is not free.  The mean time increases from
$95.0$~s for Case~2 to $174.8$~s for the corrected method, a factor of
$1.84$.  The measured direction/tangent-construction portion alone averages
$68.0$~s over the in-domain points.  It would therefore be misleading to

from this experiment.

\section{Effect of PROM Coefficient-Data Enrichment}
\label{sec:euclidean-prom-enrichment}
The nested campaign tests how much of the baseline discrepancy can be
reduced by extending parameter-space coverage at fixed architecture.  The
Euclidean basis, network architectures, two parameter-validation trajectories,
four online evaluation points, and full-residual PROM formulations are fixed.
Only the number and location of linear-PROM coefficient-training trajectories
change through the nested budgets $9$, $9+8$, $9+12$, and $9+18$.

\subsection{Offline regression accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:euclidean-prom-enrichment-training} reports errors in the
reduced coordinates used to train and select the networks.  Every learned
family has substantially smaller external parameter-validation error at
$9+12$ and $9+18$ than at baseline.  The progression is not strictly
monotone for POD--DL--ROM, whose error changes from $0.433\%$ at $9+8$ to
$0.453\%$ at $9+12$.  The validation error of the master parameter--time map shared by
Case~2 and POD--NN--ROM decreases from $4.261\%$ to $1.607\%$ with $9+8$,
to $1.137\%$ with $9+12$, and to $0.990\%$ with $9+18$.  This monotone
held-out improvement does not,
however, imply monotone accuracy at a more distant extrapolation point.
The four Euclidean master maps use the same wide SiLU architecture, raw MSE
objective, external validation data, and random seed.  Their selected
checkpoints occur at epochs 1010, 1634, 1253, and 1731 for the baseline,
$9+8$, $9+12$, and $9+18$ data, respectively, under the same
validation-based early-stopping rule; none is the result of a wall-time
interruption.

the added trajectories is therefore as important as their count.

\section{Conclusions}
The Euclidean-POD PROM campaign gives a common full-residual comparison of
three learned closures, an intrusive decoder, and two direct
reduced-coordinate maps.  The linear PROM provides an accuracy reference in
the fixed 151-coordinate trial representation.  Case~3 and PROM--POD--AE
retain close in-domain state accuracy while solving a low-dimensional online
variable.  The Case--2/POD--NN shared-map study gives a precise interpretation
of intrusive correction: solving progressively more coordinates through the
residual moves the result from the direct-map endpoint toward linear PROM, but
the prescribed tail can still transfer its error into the solved block.

The residual-adaptive extension provides a different response to that
limitation.  With the unchanged baseline network and three additional
secondary amplitudes, the common-initialization comparison reduces the
mean in-domain state error from $1.617\%$ to $0.447\%$, close to the
linear reference.  Its measured mean online cost is $1.84$ times that of
uncorrected Case~2, but lower than the measured costs of Cases~1 and~3.
The construction is a local residual-based correction, not an HDM-error
minimization principle or a guarantee of improvement for arbitrary data.

The nested enrichment study shows that $9+12$ already reaches the
linear-reference scale for four of the six learned models and removes the
localized master-map extrapolation failure observed at $9+8$.  Increasing to
$9+18$ remains useful for Case~2 and POD--NN--ROM in domain and brings all
learned models close to the linear-PROM reference at the reported points.
The comparison demonstrates that the geometry of added samples matters in
addition to their number.

The results intentionally do not mix PROM evidence with an unavailable HPROM
data set.  The ECM derivation establishes the required model-specific
construction: an HPROM rule must be built from each learned trial map and its
tangent, rather than borrowed from a linear HPROM or from a different closure.
The next HPROM campaign can therefore use the same reporting order, tables,
and model comparisons as the PROM study.

\appendix
\section{LSPG-Sensitive Basis Exploration}
\label{app:lspg-sensitive}
Before adopting the Euclidean POD basis used in the main text, we explored an
$M$-regularized basis metric intended to emphasize sensitivity of the LSPG
normal equations to reduced-coordinate perturbations.  The objective was to
reduce transfer from a learned secondary block to residual-solved primary
coordinates without degrading the linear reference.  In the present Burgers
campaign, controlled tests did not establish a systematic improvement
sufficient to justify making that metric the main experimental variable.  For
clarity and reproducibility, every reported result therefore uses the standard
Euclidean POD basis of \eqref{eq:euclidean-pod}.  The metric-aware
construction is retained as an exploratory direction, not as a contribution
or as a source of the numerical comparisons in this paper.  The historical
LSPG-sensitive enrichment campaign also selected an ELU master map, whereas
the Euclidean campaign uses SiLU.  Its earlier extrapolation trend is therefore
not an architecture-controlled metric comparison and cannot be attributed to
the basis choice alone.
